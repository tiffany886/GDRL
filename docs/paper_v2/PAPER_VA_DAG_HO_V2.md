## Abstract

Post-disaster relief relies on a small number of UAVs that provide wireless access and relay computation to low-Earth-orbit (LEO) satellites whose visibility is intermittent. A terminal can use a UAV only while it remains inside the UAV's hard-coverage disk; a task that is generated by a rescue application is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) whose nodes must execute in topological order. This paper studies how to embed such dependency-constrained scheduling into the UAV-LEO serving loop when trajectories are fixed rather than learned. We propose VA-DAG-HO, in which (i) three UAVs perform deterministic strip patrols over the rescue hotspot bands, (ii) a coverage-aware greedy association may deliberately leave a terminal unassociated when it is out of coverage, and (iii) a schedule-embedded heuristic places the ready nodes of every application on either a UAV or a relay LEO, pruning at decision time every LEO placement that cannot be finished inside one contiguous visibility window. In a simulator with 40 terminals, 3 UAVs, 2 LEOs, spatially inhomogeneous arrivals and a 2 km x 2 km disaster cell, VA-DAG-HO keeps about 25% of terminal-slots unassociated, reaches a success rate of 0.731 and an effective latency of 3.79 s, and outperforms an end-to-end flat reinforcement-learning offloading baseline by +0.13 in success rate (paired Wilcoxon p<0.01, 10 seeds) with a 35% lower effective latency. Removing the visibility prune preserves the success rate but significantly raises latency (+0.17 s, p=0.014). An ablation that treats dependent tasks as independent packages inflates the success rate to 0.818, yet commits, per completed application, about 3.3 nodes before their required inputs exist; enforcing the DAG order is therefore what makes the reported completions physically meaningful.

**Index Terms** — UAV networks, LEO satellite backhaul, DAG task scheduling, post-disaster communications, offloading, visibility windows.

## I. Introduction

After an earthquake or a flood, the access backbone is frequently destroyed while survivors and rescue teams concentrate around a few relief points [1]. A small fleet of UAVs is a standard response: they are deployed as aerial base stations that provide coverage, connectivity, and, increasingly, edge computation [2], [3]. Yet a UAV is limited in both coverage radius and energy, and it still needs a backhaul to a core network that may no longer exist. In such scenarios, low-Earth-orbit satellites constitute a survivable backhaul, because they are the last mile that does not depend on ground infrastructure [4]; their drawback is that a given satellite is visible only during short, periodic windows [5].

We consider a cell in which a fixed set of terminals requests rescue-oriented applications. Each application is not a single job but a small DAG: a chain or a fork-join pipeline such as collect-detect-fuse-decide. The computation can be executed either on the UAV that currently serves the terminal or on a LEO satellite after relaying the input through that UAV. Three constraints dominate the problem. First, coverage is hard: a terminal can associate with a UAV only when its horizontal distance to that UAV does not exceed a coverage radius R, so a non-negligible fraction of terminals is periodically unreachable. Second, visibility is intermittent: relaying a node to a LEO and executing it must fit inside one contiguous visibility window; outside a window the satellite capacity is zero. Third, the DAG structure must be respected: a child node may only start after its parents have produced their outputs.

Most prior UAV-LEO offloading studies keep every terminal reachable with some (small) rate and assume the satellite is always available [6]-[8]; and most learning-based studies make the UAV trajectory part of the learned policy [9], [10]. Neither assumption holds in our target scenario. The question we answer is therefore structural: given fixed, non-learned trajectories, how should the DAG nodes of arriving applications be placed on {UAV, relay-LEO, wait} under hard coverage and intermittent visibility?

We make three contributions.

- **C1 (embedding scheduling).** We keep the UAV motion rule-based (strip patrol) and embed the DAG scheduling into the serving loop: a coverage-aware greedy association is allowed to output "unassociated"; the ready set of each application is computed in topological order; nodes are placed on the UAV or on a relay LEO according to a deadline-aware urgency rule with explicit per-asset busy tracking.

- **C2 (decision-time visibility-window pruning).** We forbid a LEO placement whenever its relay plus service interval cannot fit inside one contiguous predicted visibility window. We show experimentally that disabling this prune keeps the success rate unchanged but significantly increases completion delay, because the scheduler commits doomed cross-window work that would otherwise have been rejected and that jams the satellite queue.

- **C3 (structure over flat RL).** Under identical fixed patrol trajectories, embedding scheduling is significantly better than end-to-end flat RL policies that learn only the offload decisions: about +0.13-0.16 success rate (paired p<0.01) and about 35% lower effective latency. The advantage is robust to the trajectory (it reappears under a backlog-chasing mover), so it is the embedding structure, not the motion pattern, that produces the gain.

The remainder is organized as follows. Section II formalizes the scenario, the channel and energy models, and the objective. Section III details VA-DAG-HO. Section IV describes the evaluation protocol, including baselines, metrics, and statistics. Section V reports the main results, the sensitivity analysis, and the ablations, and Section VI discusses the mechanism-level evidence and the limits of the claims. Section VII positions the work, and Section VIII concludes.

## II. System Model and Problem Formulation

### A. Scenario and entities

A 2 km x 2 km disaster cell contains N=40 terminals (50% static, the rest moving at up to 2 m/s), K=3 UAVs flying at 100 m altitude with a maximum speed of 20 m/s, and L=2 LEO satellites at 550 km. Time is slotted (1 s) over a horizon T=50 s. There is no ground core, no charging, and no direct terminal-to-satellite link: all backhaul passes through a UAV.

### B. Task arrivals

Requests arrive according to a spatially inhomogeneous Poisson process. Two rescue hotspots of radius 300 m are placed at the lower and upper parts of the cell; inside a hotspot the arrival rate is λ_high = 0.2 requests/s and outside it is λ_low = 0.03 requests/s. Every terminal can hold at most two pending applications, and every application has a deadline of 12 s.

### C. Application DAGs

Each application is a DAG drawn from three templates (a 4-node light chain, a 5-node fork-join, and a 4-node heavy chain) with weights 0.45/0.30/0.25. Each node i of the DAG is characterized by its required input bits in_bits(i), its compute cycles c(i), and its output bits out_bits(i). The input bits of the source node come from the terminal; for every other node, in_bits(i) equals the sum of the out_bits of its parents, which is exactly the data that a real system must wait for. Node i may start only after all of its parents have finished (topological-order constraint) and after its input has reached the executing device.

### D. Coverage and association

Let p_k(t) and x_n(t) denote the horizontal positions of UAV k and terminal n. UAV k can serve terminal n at time t only if ||x_n(t) - p_k(t)|| <= R, with R = 800 m in the frozen configuration. Let r_nk be the terminal-UAV Shannon rate (3GPP-style elevation-dependent LoS/NLoS model [11]); entries with n outside the coverage of k are set to zero and excluded from the candidate set. Association is a greedy rate-ordered assignment with a per-UAV capacity of ceil(N/K) terminals, and it is allowed to leave a terminal unassociated (a(t) = -1). This is the crucial difference with "soft-connection" models: an uncovered terminal simply has no uplink at all, so a pending application whose terminal is unassociated cannot make progress in that slot.

### E. UAV-LEO channel and visibility windows

The UAV-LEO rate uses the same LoS/NLoS model, capped at a satellite capacity of 10 Mb/s. The LEO capacity is gated by visibility: satellite l is visible in periodic windows (period 22 s, duty 0.4); the two satellites are phase-shifted (0 and 11 s) so that their windows interleave. Outside a visibility window the capacity is hard zero. To execute node i of the application pinned to UAV k on satellite l, the UAV must relay the node input to l during a window, and l must then serve the node within the same contiguous window; a relay that ends after the window closes is not usable.

### F. Energy

Each UAV carries a battery (50 kJ, low-battery threshold at 20%). Per slot we charge propulsion (hover power 80 W plus a speed-cubed drag term), computing power 150 W, and communication power 20 W. There is no charging; a UAV that depletes its battery only slows down, but low-battery states are penalized in the learning baselines' reward. Energy is not an optimization variable of the scheduler; it is reported and compared as a resource dimension.

### G. Metrics and objective

For one episode with A arrived applications, D of which complete before their deadline, we report:

- success rate S = D / A;
- conditional delay of successful applications, \bar d_cond = (Σ delay_done)/D;
- effective latency, d_eff = (Σ delay_done + F * 12 s)/A, where F = A - D is the number of failed applications. d_eff penalizes every failure by the full deadline, which removes the selection bias of \bar d_cond (a method that fails the long tasks looks fast on the successful ones);
- UAV energy consumption, the number of invalid LEO attempts, and the unassociated fraction (mean over slots of terminals with a(t) = -1).

The primary objective is to maximize the success rate within the deadline under the coverage and visibility constraints, subject to acceptable energy and latency.

## III. Proposed Method: VA-DAG-HO

VA-DAG-HO combines a fixed patrol trajectory, a coverage-aware association, and an embedded DAG scheduler with visibility pruning. Nothing in the method is learned; the comparison with learned baselines is made in Section IV.

### A. Strip patrol (T-patrol)

The cell is divided into K horizontal bands of equal width; UAV k oscillates back and forth inside band k at maximum speed along a lane in the middle of the band (deterministic and stateless across episodes). The two rescue hotspots are placed in the lower and upper bands so that the patrol sweeps both. T-patrol is deliberately task-agnostic: it does not use the instantaneous backlog or task distribution, which fits the reconnaissance-and-search workflows of the early response phase. For comparison we also run B3-hover (UAVs stay at fixed uniform positions), B2-chase (each UAV flies toward the weighted centroid of its unserved backlog), and B1-random (random walk with the same speed envelope).

### B. Coverage-aware association

At every slot, after the UAVs have moved, the environment (a) builds the (N,K) rate matrix, (b) zeroes the rates of every (n,k) pair whose horizontal distance exceeds R, (c) sorts the surviving pairs by rate, and (d) greedily assigns terminals up to the per-UAV capacity. Terminals with no feasible UAV receive a(t) = -1 and are counted in the unassociated fraction; pending applications of such terminals are frozen for the slot and, if they miss their deadline while uncovered, are counted as coverage failures (fail_uncovered).

### C. Embedded DAG scheduling (ScheduleDAG)

At every slot, given the UAV positions and the current visibility, the scheduler proceeds as follows.

1. *Ready set.* For each pending application, node i is ready if it has not been placed and, if the topological-order flag is on, all of its parents are done. Source nodes are ready immediately upon registration with the associated UAV.
2. *Priority.* Ready nodes are sorted by an urgency key: nodes whose remaining time to the deadline is below a margin (5 s) first, then heavy nodes before light ones, and among equals the earlier deadline and then the heavier workload first.
3. *Placement.* For each ready node in priority order, the scheduler evaluates the candidate devices: the pinned UAV k (the data path terminal -> UAV k -> compute on k) and the relay satellites l (terminal -> UAV k -> relay to l -> compute on l). A UAV placement is feasible if the uplink plus the compute finishes before the deadline and the UAV compute queue is free at that time. A satellite placement is feasible only if the relay interval and the service interval fit into one contiguous visibility window of l (Section II-E). If no candidate is feasible the node waits for the next slot. When multiple devices are feasible the one with the earliest finish time is chosen, with a tie-break in favor of the UAV.
4. *Bookkeeping.* Placed nodes reserve their device busy times; nodes whose reserved finish passes are marked done within the same slot; an application completes when its sink node finishes before its deadline. A slot may iterate (mark done -> compute ready -> place) until no progress is possible.

The scheduler is deterministic, has no trained parameters, and never violates the topological order by construction: the ready-set step is the only place where nodes enter the placement stage.

### D. Decision-time visibility prune (VW-prune)

The prune is the difference between the scheduler described above and the "always-on" scheduler used by baseline B6. B6 decides without looking at the windows: it computes a nominal relay-and-service schedule, and only at execution time discovers that the interval crosses an outage; that placement is committed as doomed, consumes the satellite relay resources, and the application eventually fails at its deadline. VA-DAG-HO instead rejects the placement at decision time whenever no single window can contain relay plus service. The experimental consequence is that both variants reach the same success rate (0.744 vs 0.731, not significant) but B6's completed applications are delayed by +0.17 s (p=0.014, paired), because doomed work unnecessarily occupies relay and satellite resources that would otherwise serve succeeding applications.

### E. Complexity

Each slot places at most a few dozen nodes, each against K + L devices, and the window check scans at most a small number of visibility windows per satellite; the per-slot cost is dominated by the rate-matrix computations that any serving policy must perform. The method is therefore deployable on the UAVs' embedded computers, which is relevant to the discussion in Section VI.

## IV. Baselines, Evaluation Setup, and Statistical Protocol

### A. Baselines

- **B1-random / B2-chase / B3-hover**: fixed trajectories of Section III-A, all combined with the same embedded scheduler.
- **B4-flat**: MAPPO-style shared-parameter policy whose actor learns only the offload decisions {UAV, LEO, wait} for every (UAV, terminal-slot); the velocity head is inert, and the UAVs follow exactly the same T-patrol as VA-DAG-HO.
- **B5-SAC-flat**: the off-policy counterpart of B4 (SAC with a Gumbel reparameterized discrete offload head, self-sustaining exploration), with the same T-patrol motion.
- **B6-noVWprune**: the T-patrol scheduler of VA-DAG-HO with the decision-time window prune disabled (Section III-D).

Ablations (each five seeds): w/o VW-prune (=B6), w/o DAG-order (dependent nodes treated as independent packages), w/o hard coverage (R unbounded, i.e., the soft-connection model), and w/o embedding (=B4).

### B. Configuration and reproducibility

All numbers are obtained from a single frozen configuration (`main_v2.yaml`, R=800 m, λ_high=0.2, hotspot centers fixed); a SHA-256 of this configuration, the git revision, the wall time, and the produced files are appended to a run ledger for every experiment. The simulator ships with a 45-test pytest suite (all green) that locks the semantics used here, including the zero-dependency-violation invariant of legal schedules.

### C. Statistical protocol

Scene randomness is controlled by a scene seed. Deterministic rows (VA-DAG-HO and the trajectory baselines) run one episode per scene seed. The RL rows train on train seed 100 + s and are evaluated on scene seed s, so every method is paired by scene seed. Headline claims use n=10 paired one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests at the center cell (R=800, λ_high=0.2); the sensitivity grid (Section V-C) uses five seeds per cell; each learning baseline is trained for 250 episodes per seed (B4) or 400 episodes (B5), and a budget check retrains B4 for 500 episodes.

## V. Results

### A. Main comparison

Table I reports the five-seed main comparison at the frozen operating point. VA-DAG-HO reaches a success rate of 0.731 with an effective latency of 3.79 s. Its success rate is statistically indistinguishable from B2-chase (0.745, p=1.0 at n=10), which is the strong task-aware trajectory; we do not claim patrol is better than chase, and we discuss this in Section VI. Against the flat RL baselines the gap is large and structural: B4 reaches 0.571 and B5 0.597, i.e., VA-DAG-HO is +0.13 to +0.16 higher, with a 35% lower effective latency (3.79 s vs 5.84 s for B4). B6, which shares the success rate of VA-DAG-HO, is significantly slower (Section V-B). VA-DAG-HO consumes 21.2 kJ of UAV energy, more than hover (15.3 kJ, which also completes fewer applications) but less than B4 (24.7 kJ) and comparable to B5 (22.3 kJ). Every method keeps about 25% of terminal-slots unassociated, confirming that the coverage constraint is active for all of them.

### B. Significance and training budget (center cell)

At the center cell, with ten paired scene seeds, VA-DAG-HO improves success over hover by +0.0949 (p=0.0010) and over B4 by +0.1306 (p=0.0010); B6 is slower by +0.172 s (p=0.0137) at equal success. VA-DAG-HO and chase do not differ in success (p=1.0) nor in effective latency, but chase consumes 2.1 kJ less energy (p=0.0098); we read this as chase being the higher-information baseline, not as patrol being better.

Doubling B4's budget from 250 to 500 episodes changes its success rate by -0.001 (p=0.81): the flat RL baseline is on its plateau, so its weakness relative to the embedding is structural rather than an under-training artifact. The training curves of B4 and B5 (Fig. fig_v2_train_curves.png, one training seed, evaluated every 25-50 episodes on the five scene seeds) flatten or decline by the end of the training budget: B4 hovers around 0.50-0.55 after episode 150, B5 peaks near 0.64 at episode 150 and then recedes; the annotated 500-episode evaluation of B4 confirms the plateau (p=0.81 against 250 episodes).

### D. Ablations

Table III reports the four ablations. Three results deserve emphasis.

1. **Hard coverage is what makes the problem hard.** Removing it (unbounded R) raises success to 0.875 and drives the unassociated fraction to zero: every terminal is always reachable and the problem degenerates to the easier soft-connection setting. The hard-coverage regime is therefore not an obstacle to be tuned away; it is the core of the scenario.
2. **The visibility prune is a delay mechanism, not a success mechanism.** B6 keeps the success rate but pays +0.17 s of delay and higher energy; the 745 invalid LEO attempts blocked at decision time by VA-DAG-HO become, under B6, 264 doomed commits that occupy relay resources before failing.
3. **The DAG order is what makes completions valid.** When dependent nodes are treated as independent packages, the success rate jumps to 0.818, but the scheduler then commits children before their parents' outputs exist: 276.2 ± 10.0 dependency violations per episode, i.e., about 3.3 per completed application. In a real system such an application would be returned "completed" without ever having received its required inputs. Legal schedules keep this counter at zero, and we therefore report 0.731 as the truthful success rate of the embedding.

### E. Mechanism evidence

Four replays (deterministic, five scene seeds pooled) provide the mechanism behind the numbers.

1. **The difficulty is located at the hotspots.** Split arrivals by whether the terminal lies inside a hotspot. Inside hotspots, hover reaches 0.515 success, while VA-DAG-HO reaches 0.760 (2.18 s delay); outside, both already do well (0.704-0.732). The strip patrol sweeps the two hotspot bands, which hover's fixed uniform positions cannot do. This is the mechanism behind the headline hover gap, and it is why we call the two hotspot positions "rescue points" rather than an adversarial layout: the difficulty is spatial, and the trajectory is designed for that geometry.
2. **Disconnection is structural.** The unassociated fraction stays around 0.25 across the whole episode for every mover (mean +/- std over seeds); it is not a transient of a particular trajectory.
3. **Selection bias is visible in the delay distribution.** The CDF of successful-application delays shows hover's mass at shorter delays, yet hover's effective latency is worse because about 35% of its tasks fail (the long ones). Success rate and effective latency must be read jointly, never conditional delay alone.
4. **A coverage snapshot** (seed 2, mid-episode) shows the three UAVs on their patrol lanes, the 800 m disks, the two hotspot bands, and about a quarter of the terminals outside every disk.

## VI. Discussion: What the Numbers Do and Do Not Claim

**Chase and the choice of the headline trajectory.** B2-chase, which flies toward the weighted centroid of unserved backlog, reaches 0.745 success with 2.1 kJ less energy than VA-DAG-HO. Chase is a high-information heuristic: it requires instantaneous knowledge of where the load is. T-patrol requires no such prior and matches the search-and-reconnaissance phase, when the task distribution is not yet known. We do not claim patrol is better than chase. Instead we report both, and we show that the *embedding* contribution is independent of the trajectory choice: under a chase mover, embedding beats the flat RL baseline by +0.174 success (p=0.031, paired) with -1.8 s conditional and -2.1 s effective latency, matching the +0.13-0.16 gap under patrol. The structural conclusion of the paper (C3) therefore does not depend on which fixed trajectory is used.

**Coverage radius.** Disk-coverage models with radii of a few hundred meters are standard in post-disaster UAV literature [1]-[3]. In our 2 km cell, R=800 m leaves about 25% of terminal-slots disconnected, and the sensitivity analysis shows the conclusions hold for R in [600, 900] m; R=800 is a mid-region operating point, not a tuned endpoint.

**Assumptions that bound the claims.** (i) There is no direct terminal-satellite link and no cloud outside the cell: the surviving backhaul is the UAV-to-LEO relay. Adding a direct access option is future work. (ii) The visibility schedule is deterministic and predictable from the ephemeris, and the prune uses the same schedule at decision time as the execution model. (iii) The energy budget (50 kJ per UAV) and the single-cell geometry bound the scale of the conclusions; scaling to more terminals or multiple cells is left for future work. (iv) All RL comparisons use the same scene seeds for training and evaluation pairing; training seeds are offset to avoid accidental scene leakage.

## VIII. Conclusion

We presented VA-DAG-HO, a fixed-patrol, hard-coverage, visibility-window-aware DAG scheduler for post-disaster UAV-LEO offloading. With 40 terminals, three UAVs and two intermittent LEOs, embedding the scheduling decisions into the serving loop beats flat reinforcement-learning offloading by about +0.13 success rate (paired, p<0.01) with a 35% lower effective latency, and decision-time visibility pruning yields a statistically significant delay reduction without hurting the success rate. Ablations show that the hard-coverage constraint (about 25% of terminal-slots unassociated) and the topological-order enforcement (zero violations under legal scheduling) are precisely what make these numbers physically meaningful: without hard coverage the problem softens, and without the DAG order the "extra" completions start before their inputs exist. The mechanism is localized at the rescue hotspots, which a task-agnostic strip patrol reaches by design, and the embedding advantage is robust to the choice of the fixed trajectory.
